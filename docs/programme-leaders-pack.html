The mathematics strand

H₂O — Towards 2040 · Year 8 · pack for programme leaders · 16/08/2026

Six periods of the Year 8 end-of-year programme, spent on bivariate data — how two measurements about water move together, and what you may honestly say about the future because of it. It runs as a self-directed website on the students' own laptops, and four of the six lessons need no mathematics teacher in the room.

If you read nothing else

  • It is a website, not an app. Nothing to install, no account to create, no licence to buy. Students open a link and sign in with their school ID.
  • No student data leaves the laptop. There is no server, no database, no analytics and no email address anywhere in it. See Student data overleaf.
  • Four lessons are supervised by whoever is free. Every room comes with crib notes written for an adult who does not teach mathematics, and a printed sheet.
  • Each crew produces one Data Evidence Card, a shared file in the crew's own Teams folder, printable at any stage of completion.
  • Six things are still needed from the programme. They are listed on the last page and none of them is large.

What students actually do

Every crew asks its own question about water — shower length against litres used, roof area against water collected — then measures it themselves at home, charts it, and makes one prediction from it. The mathematics is ordinary Year 8 statistics. What is unusual is that the data is theirs, so a sloppy method produces a chart that visibly does not work. That is a far better argument than being told to be careful.

Lesson What happens Badge
1 · Getting started Twenty minutes with the whole cohort, then a room. A newspaper claim about water is put up and the cohort is invited to prove it wrong. Types of data, what a point on a chart means. Each crew chooses its research question. —
2 · Reader Reading charts other people made. Naming the variables and their units, saying what one point means, describing a relationship. Nothing is made today. Reader
3 · Collector What makes a measurement fair. Students pull apart a supplied recording sheet that has gone wrong, then plan their own and start collecting. Measuring happens between lessons, at home. Collector
4 · Plotter Choosing the right chart for the data, labelling axes properly, and the same six numbers drawn on three different scales — only one of which is honest. Two charts go onto the crew Card. Plotter
5 · Predictor A line of best fit drawn by hand through their own points, read as a sentence about water rather than about algebra, then one prediction inside the data and one beyond it — with how much to trust each. Predictor
6 · The whole story Twenty minutes with the whole cohort: the Lesson 1 claim is taken apart, twice over. Then each crew is shown its own Lesson 2 answer from four weeks earlier and asked what it was worth. Cards are finished, swapped, checked and printed. —

Lessons 1 and 6 open and close with twenty minutes for the whole cohort; Lessons 2 to 5 are self-directed from start to finish. Why two lessons are un-badged. Lessons 1 and 6 are the frame, not the assessment. A student who says something honest in Lesson 6 about a mistake made in Lesson 2 has done the most valuable thing in the unit, and putting a badge on it would turn it into a performance.

Student data — what leaves the laptop

Nothing. This is worth stating plainly because it is the question that usually gets asked last and should be asked first.

How it works

  • A static website over HTTPS. There is no server behind it that can receive anything.
  • Students sign in with their school ID and a low-stakes password, checked against a list that ships with the site. The ID is stored as a one-way hash, the password stretched and salted per person.
  • Work is saved in the browser on the student's own laptop — and, for the crew Card only, in a file inside the school's own Teams or OneDrive.
  • Progress exports to a file and imports on another laptop.

What there is none of

  • No email addresses — not in the site, not in the roll, nowhere.
  • No analytics, telemetry, tracking or third-party scripts of any kind.
  • No database, no cloud storage account, no vendor, no cookies.
  • No marks or answers held centrally. The answer key is on paper, with staff.
  • No student names or student work in the published site or its source.

The password is deliberately low-stakes — a word about water, not a secret. It stops two students opening each other's work by guessing a school ID, and nothing sensitive sits behind it. Students are told so, and told not to reuse a password that matters.

What the supervising adult has to do

Four lessons run without a mathematics teacher. That is a design decision, not a staffing compromise: every question has a four-rung hint ladder and the last rung gives the answer — deliberately, and without penalty. A student who needs all four hints earns the same badge as one who needed none.

They are given

  • A supervisor screen listing, for each room, what it asks for, what good enough looks like as something visible on a screen, and one sentence to read aloud to a student who is stuck.
  • A printed sheet with the same crib notes, the badge rules, and the answers.
  • Buttons to open a locked room, move a student's work to another laptop, or start a laptop again.

They are never asked to

  • Judge whether a piece of mathematics is correct, or clever.
  • Mark anything.
  • Know what a line of best fit is.

Before the programme runs, one non-mathematician will be handed the supervisor screen with no briefing and asked "is this crew's work good enough?" for all six rooms. Anywhere they hesitate, that crib note gets rewritten. Nominations welcome.

The film strand

Every chart tool exports a 1920 × 1080 image with a white background and axis labels set at a size that survives being filmed off a screen. Crews are shown where that button is. Whether a chart appears in the film is not a mathematics criterion and is not assessed here: the strand supplies exports that make it easy and stops there, and whoever owns the film rubric owns the checking. It is stated on the Card and in the Lesson 6 room so that no crew reaches the edit believing otherwise.

Access, and who this is written for

  • Touch and keyboard, equally. Every drag — sorting cards, placing a line of best fit — also has a keyboard route.
  • Charts never carry meaning in colour alone. Shape, label or position always carries it as well.
  • Everything prints, and a missed lesson needs no catch-up. A student who cannot get online works on paper; a student who misses a lesson works through that room in their own time.
  • Plain language, defined once. Every term is defined where it first appears, then used consistently. No synonyms for words already taught.

What the strand needs from the programme

Six things, none of them large

  • A Teams or OneDrive folder per crew, which the crew can write to. The Data Evidence Card lives there. Without it the crews have no shared artefact.
  • Crew names and how many crews. The site currently offers placeholders.
  • The programme start and end dates, and a name and title to sign the certificates.
  • Confirmation of which four lessons have no mathematics teacher in the room. The build assumes Lessons 2 to 5.
  • Printing — two sheets per crew Card, plus recording sheets in Lesson 3 and certificates at the end.
  • One non-mathematician for fifteen minutes, to test the supervisor screen as described above.

What is still open

Everything in this pack is built and deployed. Two things are still unresolved, and neither changes what happens in a lesson. Whether the fleet keeps browser storage across a restart is being tested on a school laptop this week; if not, progress moves onto the crew file instead — a change behind the scenes only. The roll currently holds 110 students and 87 staff; if the cohort is larger, the rest are needed before the first lesson, because a student who is not on the list cannot open the door.

One thing deliberately not treated as a risk: the locks on the rooms are thin, and a student who reads the page source could work out that a room opens another way. Accepted — a student set on skipping the mathematics will do so whatever the site does, and one who is stuck deserves a fourth hint more than the room deserves a stronger lock.
