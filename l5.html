What you are doing today

Today you draw a line through a scatterplot and use it to say what has not been measured. That is what a prediction is, and it is how anyone gets from the water we use now to the water we will use in 2040.

There are three questions to answer. Each one gives you a piece of a code.

Take as long as you need. If a question will not come, press I need a hint. The hints get more helpful each time, and the last one tells you the answer. You still get your piece of the code.

Four words you need

A line of best fit is a straight line drawn through a scatterplot. It passes as close as it can to all the points. Your worksheets call this the line of good fit. It is the same line.

The gradient tells you how much the RV changes for every one unit the EV goes up. You work it out from two points on the line: the change in the RV divided by the change in the EV.

Interpolation is reading the line inside the measurements you have. Extrapolation is reading it beyond them.

Your three questions

Now do it with your own data

Open your scatterplot in the two-variable tool, place your own line of best fit, and read your own gradient off it. Then write these four things on your crew's Card. Your badge needs three of them.

  • Our line — write it as y = mx + c, using your gradient for m.
  • What the gradient means — say it as a sentence about water, not about maths. “For every extra minute in the shower, the water used goes up by about 9 litres.”
  • Reading inside our data (interpolation) — pick a value you did not measure but could have. “At 6 minutes our line gives 70 litres.”
  • Reading past our data (extrapolation) — pick a value beyond everything you measured, then say what it is worth. “At 15 minutes our line gives 150 litres. We measured only from 2 to 11 minutes, so nothing we did tests whether the line is still straight out there.”

That last sentence is the one that matters for 2040. A prediction without it is a guess wearing a straight line.
